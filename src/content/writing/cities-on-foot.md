Whenever I land in a new city (or come back to an old one), my favorite way to get a feel for it isn’t by checking off an itinerary. It’s by lacing up my shoes and heading out the door on foot.

## Cities Are Smaller Than They Feel

Modern cities are dense, which means the neighborhoods and landmarks I actually care about are usually packed close together. A 5K or 10K route can cover a surprisingly large chunk of a city’s core. At an average walking pace of 5 km/h or a running pace of 10 km/h, a 10K takes one to two hours—long enough to cross half a dozen neighborhoods. I’m fully in the middle of the city, moving just fast enough to cover real ground and slow enough for serendipity to happen.

It’s always fun seeing how many different vibes I can pass through in a single 10K:

In Tokyo, I left behind Ebisu’s tucked-away bistros, skirted the electric rush of Shibuya, and hit the wide, tree-lined boulevard of Omotesando. Mid-run at Meiji Jingu Gaien, the city noise softened as I looped along the avenue of ginkgo trees shedding their yellow leaves onto the pavement. Turning south, the crowds thinned into the quiet, architectural calm of Minami-Aoyama before I finished my [10K](https://www.strava.com/activities/10330760758) along the residential, embassy-lined streets of Hiroo.

Back in my hometown of Guangzhou, [this 10K along the Pearl River](https://www.strava.com/activities/10261533940) starts in Yuancun’s street stalls and old apartment blocks. From there, it passes the newer high-rises of Liede, cuts across the glass-and-steel CBD of Zhujiang New Town—with Canton Tower rising across the water—and turns around on the quiet, exclusive Ersha Island.

## High Information Bandwidth

Taking a subway, bus, or taxi feels like traveling inside a sealed box—moving from point A to point B while skipping everything in between. On foot, the information bandwidth is huge. I’m forced to pay attention to the streets, the people, and the subtle transitions between neighborhoods. I see, hear, smell, and feel the city in real time.

That sensory immersion creates room for serendipity that I just can’t plan on an itinerary. A few moments I only caught because I was out running or walking:

In Tokyo, on [a morning run starting out from Ikebukuro](https://www.strava.com/activities/9084302340), I picked a random direction and stumbled into Mejiro. Just a short jog from Ikebukuro’s neon and station crowds, Mejiro felt like another world—narrow streets lined with low-rise houses, potted plants by the front doors, and quiet neighborhood alleys.

Another time in Tokyo, on [December 15, 2023—an absurdly warm winter night](https://x.com/extremetemps/status/1735828300633903154?s=20)—I went for [a night run along the Yamanote Line](https://www.strava.com/activities/10384090145). December nights there usually sit around 5°C, but that night the temperature actually climbed as it got later, going from 18°C at 10 PM to 20°C by 3 AM. Running past glowing Christmas lights and holiday music in warm, summer-like air felt completely surreal.

In Hawaii, [this run](https://www.strava.com/activities/14583822282) made me realize just how close Diamond Head actually is to Waikiki (less than 5 km round-trip to the base of the crater). Along the way, I passed small oceanfront parks filled with morning yoga groups and found a narrow seaside path connecting Makalei and Leahi Beach Parks.

In Seoul, a [5K loop around Yeouido](https://www.strava.com/activities/19732140447) showed me how naturally the city builds running into its core: wide, dedicated paths along the Han River feed straight into Yeouido Park, putting a shaded green trail right between the financial district’s skyscrapers.

Nine years after graduating from ZJU, I went back to Hangzhou over the New Year’s holiday and wandered around West Lake with my family. Going [north along Yanggong Causeway](https://www.strava.com/activities/16953327661), we mostly shared the shade with a handful of locals, passing small streams and rocks along the path. On the way [back via Su Causeway](https://www.strava.com/activities/16953327374), it was full holiday mode—tourists and snack stalls everywhere, and sightseeing buggies barely squeezing through the foot traffic.

## Earning My Appetite

Part of the joy of visiting any city is eating my way through it. When I only have a short window to explore, I naturally want to sample as much as I can—which usually means eating far more than I would at home. Getting out on foot beforehand doesn’t just connect me to the city; it creates a welcome calorie deficit, so I sit down at the table hungry and ready to enjoy every bite.

## Low Friction

Running is pure freedom. All I need is a pair of shoes. It can be at dawn before the city wakes up, on a restless summer night, or on an unhurried afternoon. There are no constraints of time or place: no right moment, no specific venue, no special gear needed. I can go from staring at a screen to out the door in two minutes, with no booking, no transit, and no plan.

## The 4 AM Jet-Lag Run

Whenever I fly back to Asia from the US, jet lag wakes me up at an ungodly hour, like 4:00 AM, long before any shops, cafes, or attractions open. Instead of staring at the ceiling, I lace up my shoes and head out while the streets are still empty (that [4:59 AM Ikebukuro run](#ikebukuro-run) above was one of those mornings). Then it’s back for a hot shower and a slow local breakfast. At home, my morning routine is usually just two eggs and a coffee; on the road, a sunrise run lets me enjoy a full feast before the rest of the city even wakes up. It’s easily my favorite way to start a trip.
